# Sponsor interview: soft sponsor

- **Basis:** real conversations, anonymised composite
- **Format:** one-on-one interview, excerpt
- **Date:** Autumn
- **Interviewee:** S (she/her), supports the move to AI for the product and project roles in a large organisation, together with the team leads; no line responsibility for anyone in the group
- **Interviewer:** I

---

**I:** How did this land on your desk?

**S:** It's simply my role. Together with the team leads I look at what the AI change does to people and to the way they work. Leadership gave everyone the tools and said, go and use them. Roughly.

**I:** And what happened?

**S:** With the developers, the way forward is fairly clear. They got going, more and more of them work with agents, some have built whole pipelines for themselves. They enable themselves. On the product and project side it's different. They come to me and say, what am I supposed to do with this? Apart from bouncing ideas off it or producing something quickly, where does it help me in my work?

**I:** Had they been trained?

**S:** Yes, all of them went to some external course at some point. Mostly optional, who wants to join. And they came out saying, fine, I heard it, I built myself a skill or a little agent, but I have no idea how to use any of it sensibly in my actual work. In theory the knowledge should be there. The doing isn't.

**I:** How do you know that?

**S:** From what people say. It came up again and again in conversations. I ask, can you take any of it over into your work? And the answer is no.

**I:** What do you hear, exactly?

**S:** Two versions, really. One is, I have an idea, but I don't know how. The other is, I know what the tool can do, I just can't see what to use it for in my own work. Those two keep coming back.

**I:** And what does that do to you?

**S:** I find it a bit of a shame. What I pick up is a kind of helplessness. I'd like to, but I don't know what. And honestly, I'm in the same spot. If a developer came to me, I could show them a direction. For the others I can't say, go there, do that. It's one big question mark.

**I:** What would you not want to happen?

**S:** Another training. Not another format where someone talks for four hours, builds a couple of agents on stage, and people walk out without an idea. And not starting from scratch again with what a language model is. What I fear most: people go home with a full bag of new tricks and no reason to reach for any of them, so nothing changes. Everyone enjoyed it, and that's where it ends.

**I:** Is it a question of access? Tools, licences, permissions?

**S:** No. That's not what's missing. From the very top the message is, we try things, everyone gets access, you can do whatever you like within the rules, let's see what comes of it. They're all motivated and they have plenty of access. I think what's missing is ideas, and someone who clears the obstacles out of the way. Maybe sometimes it's time.

**I:** Who would you have in the room?

**S:** That's tricky. Everyone has more or less the same job title, but the work underneath varies a lot. Requirements work, project management, some product owner tasks, some are closer to assistant roles. I asked for people who'd at least done a basics course and were keen. Several turned up who hadn't, through no fault of their own. The group won't have one level that suits everyone. One used to be a developer and has built things himself. Another prototypes together with a designer. The rest mostly use whatever AI comes built into the tools they already have. And it differs between teams as well. In some, people still experiment as they go; elsewhere a few shared helpers have settled in, though there aren't many yet.

**I:** When you picture working with someone on this, what does it look like?

**S:** Whether it's a four-hour workshop or someone who works alongside a team for a while, I don't know. Maybe it's a standard course after all, maybe shadowing a team, maybe digging out use cases together, maybe some kind of mentoring. And I can't even say whether it's one thing or several: a single session, a first session plus something that follows, or something else again. I have no idea.

**I:** If you had to slice it, how would you?

**S:** I sketched three ways. By role: take the product people, or the ones doing project or requirements work, and start from what their job is. By team: sit with a team, have them walk you through how work comes in and moves along, and look where AI could help, say with what comes out of stakeholder conversations. Or by project: take one larger initiative where several teams work together and see how the people leading it could be supported.

**I:** Which one would you pick?

**S:** I haven't. That's why I opened it up so wide. I really want to stay open, see what comes out, and then decide that one sounds like a good direction. It's also fine if the answer for some role is, there's no big use case here.

**I:** Say it goes well. What would tell you it was worth it?

**S:** For me, nothing at first. It's for them. I'd hear it. People saying, this helps me, here's where I used it, this process got simpler, we work together differently, I have more room because I could automate or simplify something. And then other teams coming over on their own and saying, that sounds exciting, we'd like to have a look, we could use that too.

Those are very soft points, I know. It's not a hard return on investment. They can work out hard goals for themselves later.

**I:** By when would you want to hear that?

**S:** I don't have a timeline. It depends on the use cases. Some things simply take longer. What I could imagine is a pilot: does the approach enable people to carry on by themselves? If it works, we call it a method and multiply it.

**I:** Who decides whether a pilot happens?

**S:** The team leads carry the business responsibility for the area. I don't manage anyone. So for that, we need the others.

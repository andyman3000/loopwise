# Sponsor interview: Ines, head of delivery, IT service provider

- **Basis:** synthetic, built from patterns in real conversations
- **Format:** one-on-one interview, full conversation
- **Date:** Autumn
- **Interviewee:** Ines Albrecht (she/her), head of delivery at an IT service provider that builds custom software for a few long-term clients; booked a multi-day format after several scoping calls, interviewed while preparing it, before it takes place
- **Interviewer:** Loopwise

---

**Interviewer:** Hi Ines. Your calendar showed a sprint review right before. Did it let you go?

**Ines:** On the dot, for once. Honestly a good one, the team showed a lot. So I'm in a decent mood, make the most of it.

**Interviewer:** I will. I'd like to go back to the start today, how the whole thing came about for you, from the first idea to now. Maybe start with your job.

**Ines:** The title is head of delivery. I'm one of three people running the company. And in practice I'm also the only product owner we have, for everything. Most of my week isn't product work at all, it's hiring, clients, money, and the product decisions still all land with me. That's the knot we're trying to untie.

**Interviewer:** How big is the company?

**Ines:** About thirty-five people. Most of them developers. I started as a developer here, eight years ago, and I've been in this role for three.

**Interviewer:** And how did a training end up on your desk?

**Ines:** We're in the middle of a change. For years this company basically had one client and one very long project. Very stable. Our advisor describes those years as: the ticket arrived finished, and you built what it said. That was the job.

**Interviewer:** And now?

**Ines:** Now there are new clients, two active, a third one smaller for now. And the developers' job has changed shape. With the coding agents, building something used to fill the week and now it's a morning. What's left is everything nobody ever taught them: what the client actually needs, what comes first, whether it did anything once it's out. That's their job now too, the whole way through. The hope is that the time we gain goes there, so part of what I do today moves to the teams. Our advisor is firmer about it than I am: whatever we become, not a place that builds to order.

**Interviewer:** Part of the product owner work.

**Ines:** Yes. I'd stay at the level of the client and the roadmap, where do they want to be next year. The teams take it from there. Break it down into epics and stories, decide what comes first within one of those, map the dependencies. Right now they can't, or not really.

**Interviewer:** What makes you say they can't?

**Ines:** We've seen it. On a smaller project, more of a learning project for us, some developers ran user interviews themselves. Which I loved. And then the results went onto one wiki page as a long list of one-line items, a few with "must" written next to them, a few with a question mark. That was the whole analysis. Nobody grouped anything, nobody said what came up again and again.

**Interviewer:** What should have happened with them?

**Ines:** That's part of why I'm here, honestly. I know a list with question marks isn't the answer. Which method, what you do first with twenty pages of notes, that's exactly the bit they're missing.

**Interviewer:** Let's go back to the beginning with us. When you first got in touch, what did you ask for?

**Ines:** A shopping list, and I wasn't even sure how many things were on it. What I really wanted was a hackathon, the team building its own automations. But I thought before that, someone has to teach them the basics of product work. And then there's the question of how all that works with agents. And maybe there's a workshop in the middle, or maybe that's just the first morning of the hackathon. I said "no idea" a lot in that first call.

**Interviewer:** So you weren't sure which of those you needed from us.

**Ines:** No. My actual question to your colleague was more or less: here's my list, which part is yours? One event or three, and in what order, that was the question I brought, not something I'd worked out.

**Interviewer:** What could you have told me?

**Ines:** What hurts. Tickets that are bad. When a ticket comes out of real conversations with users, it's usually fine. But sometimes nobody has talked to a user yet, and then the ticket is a guess written up nicely. And we've built a guess more than once. I could list examples for an hour. What to buy to fix it, no.

**Interviewer:** If it works, what would you see? How would you know?

**Ines:** Our advisor has a line for it that I've adopted: the teams should be judged by the client problems they make go away, not by how many tickets they close.

**Interviewer:** And how would you see that it happened?

**Ines:** Hm. That's the hard part. For that you need a second look once the thing is out, and someone willing to go round again when the second look is bad. Right now there's hardly anything to look at. One project went live only a few weeks ago and we're still fixing things, and the other is in a test phase where the first users move over and the rest stay on the old system. So there's no number yet where I could say, look, it moved.

**Interviewer:** Nothing at all you measure?

**Ines:** On the teacher platform, that's the one matching substitute teachers with schools, the team is building a dashboard to see whether the matching does what we think. And there's a test with real users and interviews at the end. On the other one, a start-up building booking software for repair workshops, it's more qualitative. We sit down with users and go through whether the flows fit how they work.

**Interviewer:** And do they fit?

**Ines:** Too early to say. Our advisor's bet is that a month after it's fully in use, the people at the workshop counters will be keeping their own list on paper next to it, because the flows were thought up far away from a counter. And we'd have nothing to counter that with. No satisfaction numbers, nothing. That'll hurt.

**Interviewer:** So if I asked you for one sentence now, what success looks like for the teams' work, could you write it?

**Ines:** Not one I'd sign. Something about problems instead of tickets. But checking after delivery whether a thing worked barely happens here, and while nothing is properly live there's nothing to check it against anyway. Your colleague brought up that delivering a feature and the feature doing its job aren't the same thing. I understood the point. It still doesn't give me anything to measure.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned an advisor. Who's that?

**Ines:** A freelancer who's been with us for a bit over a year. He advises the teams, helps with strategy, whatever comes up. He'll run our own half day before your part, the one about why any of this matters.

**Interviewer:** Why did you split it like that?

**Ines:** Because of the first outline you sent. He went through it and marked a lot of it up for me. His view was: good training, but written for people who already think like product people and need the tools. Ours are the reverse. Give them a new tool and they're using it by lunch, they're very sharp. What they're missing is the reasoning behind it.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example of that?

**Ines:** His standard one: hand our developers ten features and they start with the first and work down. Which of the ten the client cares about most, which one can't start before another is done, nobody stops to ask. To a product person that's the first thing you do. For the developers it isn't even a question.

**Interviewer:** So the first draft didn't fit your group.

**Ines:** It took too much for granted. Your colleague said something similar later, that plenty of people with "product manager" in their title turn out to be mostly managing tickets, and that the gap with developers tends to be bigger.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the group. Who's going to be in the room?

**Ines:** Around ten. Twelve if two people from the neighbouring team join. Mostly developers across the two, two and a half projects. Our product designer, she came to us earlier this year for UX work and is growing into product now. A UX working student. And one developer who mostly keeps an older client's system running, I still haven't decided whether he comes.

**Interviewer:** And how different are they?

**Ines:** Very, if you ask our advisor. He sees the teams day to day more than I do. In one team, he says, there's a developer who wants every ticket fully specified before he starts, every dependency sorted out, and in the other team you give them a title and off they go. Some prioritise well without any method and others need it from the start. One team works pretty freely with the agents, just prompting, the other has built a proper set of shared skills. From where I sit it's simpler: one or two people we still have to chase a bit. We're on it.

**Interviewer:** Does that spread worry you for the format?

**Ines:** Less than it worries him. Underneath, I think both teams need much the same process. Your colleague's question was a different one: whether there's a single example everyone can work on.

**Interviewer:** What did you suggest?

**Ines:** Our hackathon idea. We record a lot of our meetings, there's a small internal tool for that. But after every client meeting the follow-up is still manual, someone has to remember to write the summary, change the ticket, fix the wiki page, ask the client what they thought. So the idea was to automate that chain, things I could use as product owner.

**Interviewer:** And?

**Ines:** He pushed back. Politely. He said that's already execution, we've found the problem and half designed the fix. For practising discovery he wanted something earlier. A small business problem that's still open, where we don't know yet which solution is right.

**Interviewer:** How did that land?

**Ines:** Fair point. I hadn't looked at it that way. I thought I was bringing a good example and it was the wrong kind. So now we have to find a real one in the projects.

**Interviewer:** Have you got one?

**Ines:** Not yet. And that worries me more than it should. Something that's urgent for us this week can be settled next week. If I pick something now, by the workshop it may be old news. So I said I'd send it a few days before, while it's still hot.

**Interviewer:** Let's talk about the early stage once more. When you first reached out, what did you actually know? Group size, dates, that kind of thing?

**Ines:** Not much. On group size, the first number on your side was five, I think. Then I said we're about ten. Now it's ten to twelve. And that changed the plan, because your side said with that many it's worth having two trainers.

**Interviewer:** And dates?

**Ines:** They moved twice. One date clashed on your side, the week after didn't really suit anyone either. In the end it came down to our managing director, who very much wants to be there and whose calendar is the bottleneck. Plus school holidays, plus people back from leave. We settled on it a few weeks ago.

**Interviewer:** And the length?

**Ines:** I asked in the first call whether we were talking about full days or a few hours each. I had no idea. For us the hard part is pulling everyone out of the projects at once, so I wanted as few days as possible, and close together, not spread over a month.

**Interviewer:** Why close together?

**Ines:** That was my instinct, and your colleague backed it: in his experience, with two weeks between the theory and the practice people lose a lot. So our advisor put our own half day on the Thursday before, when we change sprints anyway and everyone is in the office.

**Interviewer:** And budget, how did you approach that?

**Ines:** We had hours left from earlier work with you, and at the start I honestly didn't know whether this would come out of those or be something separate. Later your side told me the hours cover a day and a half, and we're planning two days. So that's still open.

**Interviewer:** You've seen the current agenda. How does it look to you?

**Ines:** Good. Full. Your colleague asked me which block I'd cut if we run out of time, and I asked him why we'd cut anything. I want all of it. Discovery matters, the structure part too. Our advisor thinks prioritisation can be a quick pass for most people. And the ticket part we can keep short, that already comes up in our retros.

**Interviewer:** The ticket part already comes up for you. What does that look like?

**Ines:** We do break things down into stories and sometimes subtasks. But what's written in them is short, on purpose. A sentence or two on the problem, and the rest gets worked out. Because I want the developers asking users about the details instead of expecting everything to be written down for them. Not everyone likes that.

**Interviewer:** I heard there was an option to make it all theory, so everything fits.

**Ines:** Your colleague offered that, and also said it would be tough on people. I said no. There have to be exercises. A day of only listening doesn't change how anyone works afterwards.

**Interviewer:** What would it look like if it doesn't change anything?

**Ines:** Our advisor has a picture for that which stuck with me. A developer goes back to his project with a head full of methods, tells everyone the workshop was great, and never uses a single one, because nobody ever made him see what they're for. That's his biggest worry, and it's why he wanted the half day on the why first.

**Interviewer:** And after the format, what happens?

**Ines:** I expect a long list of things we'll want to set up for ourselves. The first one we start straight away, in the hackathon. Everything after that is our job, not yours. When and how we get to it has to fit next to two client projects with deadlines, and I don't have an answer yet for how that works.

**Interviewer:** Has that worked before, getting something like that into everyday work?

**Ines:** Mixed. A while ago we tried a fully automated build pipeline, spec goes in, software comes out. It half worked and we dropped it. For us the problem was timing. We were still prototyping, nothing was settled, and every step needed so much back and forth with the agent that it couldn't run on its own.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned the meeting recordings earlier. Is knowledge part of this too?

**Ines:** It's what bothers me most day to day. Ask two developers where they keep what the client decided last week and you get two answers, and neither is a place the other would look. I'm no better, my own system works for me and nobody else could use it. Say the designer changes a screen on Tuesday. Whoever didn't hear about it is still building the old one on Thursday.

**Interviewer:** Is anything shared?

**Ines:** Skills and rules, yes, the developers built a shared collection across projects. Whether everyone sticks to them is another matter. And in one project two of them built something that pulls all the sources into one place with a proper index. Our advisor's reason for it: we kept finding different statements in different places, and two people could each be sure they were following the source.

**Interviewer:** And across projects?

**Ines:** Same problem, bigger. One team spends a week choosing a payment provider, and the next team starts that research again from nothing. I'd like them to just find it and build on it, without a round of meetings. But that can wait for a second phase. Your colleague's view was to get one project's way of working right first, and I'm fine with that.

**Interviewer:** What do you want from us before the workshop?

**Ines:** Look at what we already have before you come. The skill collection, the indexing thing. One of the developers who built most of it can walk you through it in half an hour, there's nothing secret in there. Then the exercises fit our setup. And from our side, the real example, I owe you that. Probably one per project: what's next, what the client wants to reach this year, maybe a first guess at how to get there.

**Interviewer:** When can I tell my colleague to expect those?

**Ines:** Give me until next week. I'll talk to both teams first, otherwise I'm just guessing what's burning for them.

**Interviewer:** That's fair. Thanks, Ines, this was a lot.

**Ines:** Good. I'll get back to the teams then. Talk soon.

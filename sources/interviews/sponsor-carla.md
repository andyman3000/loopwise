# Sponsor interview: Carla, head of product owners, home and living retailer

- **Basis:** synthetic, built from patterns in real conversations
- **Format:** one-on-one interview, full conversation
- **Date:** Autumn
- **Interviewee:** Carla Seidel (she/her), head of product owners at a premium home and living retailer with stores and a large online shop; before any booking
- **Interviewer:** Loopwise

---

**Interviewer:** Hi Carla, thanks for making the time. Did the link work alright?

**Carla:** It did. I'm in a little side room, we've just come out of quarterly planning next door, so if I sound a bit fried, that's why.

**Interviewer:** Then we'll go easy. How did the planning go?

**Carla:** Long. Fine, actually. A lot of talk about where the teams are going with all the AI stuff, which is more or less why I wanted to talk to you anyway.

**Interviewer:** Good, that's a nice way in. Maybe first, how did you come across us?

**Carla:** Someone from my old team did one of your workshops at the company she moved to and mentioned you. That's all, nothing dramatic.

**Interviewer:** And what's your role exactly?

**Carla:** I lead the product owners. They're spread over the online shop and the systems behind the stores, and each of them has someone from the business side opposite them, the people who own that area commercially.

**Interviewer:** How long have you been doing that?

**Carla:** In this role about four years. Before that I was a product owner here myself, so I know the chair.

**Interviewer:** You said the teams are going somewhere with AI. Where are they?

**Carla:** All over the place, that's the real answer. A few teams are far ahead, they work with agents every day, it's just normal for them. And others are roughly where they were a year ago, maybe the chat tool for an email now and then. So when somebody asks me how far we are as a company, I don't know what to say. There is no "we" there. It's per team, and inside a team it's per person.

**Interviewer:** Is that spread a problem for you?

**Carla:** It's more a fact than a problem. Well. Partly a problem. Inside the fast teams you get this tension. Some developers say, I don't need to read every line the agent writes anymore, it works, move on. And others say no, if I don't understand what's in there, who keeps it running when it breaks at night.

**Interviewer:** Who's right?

**Carla:** Both, sort of? It causes friction, I won't pretend it doesn't. But I don't think it has to be either-or. You can have people who go fast and people who watch the plumbing, even in the same team.

**Interviewer:** And where are you in all this, as a leader?

**Carla:** Behind. No, seriously. If I'm honest about who's holding things up at the moment, it's the leadership layer, not the teams. Me included. What we did do is tell a handful of teams, you're allowed to go ahead, find new ways of working, you don't need permission for every experiment. My part is to actually let them. And at the same time to step back far enough to see what's really changing, and what of it the other teams could use.

**Interviewer:** That's two jobs at once.

**Carla:** Yes. And the first one is harder than it sounds. Letting go, I mean.

**Interviewer:** What does letting go look like, in practice?

**Carla:** Give them a direction, put up two or three guardrails, and then keep my hands off. At this stage I think that's what leading is. The moment I start steering details, I'm the brake.

**Interviewer:** Has the way those teams work changed much?

**Carla:** You know, I keep saying this and people look at me funny, but none of it is actually new. Small slices, testing, early feedback, we did all that before. It's just all dialled up. And the part people forget: if the building gets faster and your checking is still done by hand, you drown. The validation has to be automated as well, or nobody keeps up. We're lucky there, to be fair. The technical groundwork here was solid before any of this started, so the fast teams have something to stand on.

**Interviewer:** And the product owners in those fast teams, how are they doing?

**Carla:** That's what I'm watching. Nothing has gone wrong yet, I want to be clear. But if the developers next to you work with agents at that pace, I expect the question to come up: can the product owner still keep up? And it's not as if the job was relaxed before.

**Interviewer:** So where does the bottleneck sit, in your view?

**Carla:** Not in the building. It never did. I've been saying that for years, long before agents. The slow part is working out what we want to do at all, and then getting everyone on board who thinks we should do something else.

**Interviewer:** And that part agents won't speed up. Deciding stays at human speed.

**Carla:** Yes, I'd agree with that. What would help is a different way of working together. Clear lines on who is responsible for what. And as few people as possible who need to say yes before something moves. And not having the people who decide in one place and the people who build in another. Ideally the business side sits on the same floor as the team, at the next desk. Not two buildings and a weekly sync.

**Interviewer:** When teams get faster, do you see more coming out of them?

**Carla:** More output, sure. But is that worth anything? We agree on this quite broadly here, actually, that teams shouldn't churn out that much more. The question is whether any of it brings value.

**Interviewer:** Does that end in smaller teams at some point?

**Carla:** That's the assumption we work with, yes. So far, when we gained capacity, we moved it around: we started a new team, and some of the others got smaller to make room.

**Interviewer:** Doing what?

**Carla:** Something new on the shop side. I'd rather not go into that one yet.

**Interviewer:** Fair. Let me guess, your product owners are already building their own little AI helpers on the side, prompts, small tools?

**Carla:** Hm. I don't know, to be honest. Some, probably. It depends. What I do notice is that they don't talk about it. I think a lot of them simply have no idea where they stand. Am I ahead, am I behind the others, they can't see it. And going to the person next to you, or to me, and saying I don't get this, help me, that's a big step for many. Bigger than it should be.

**Interviewer:** Is there anywhere they share what they do?

**Carla:** There's a community format, a session every so often where a few people show what they've built. Those few are brave. Whether everybody dares to stand up there, I doubt it. That's actually one reason I'm talking to you and not just organising something in-house. If someone from outside runs it, people find it easier to say, I've never done this. Nobody from their own line is sitting there taking notes, so to speak.

**Interviewer:** Doesn't the company offer anything on AI already?

**Carla:** It does, but most of it is aimed at the developers, and product owners get lumped in with them, as if they were all technical anyway. Many of mine aren't. Some came from customer service, some from project management, a couple from logistics. Good product people, but not developers. And so I have this feeling, and it's only a feeling, I haven't measured anything, that some of the business stakeholders sitting opposite my product owners are further along with the tools now than my own people.

**Interviewer:** That's an odd inversion.

**Carla:** It is odd, yes.

**Interviewer:** Let's go to discovery for a moment. How do your teams work out what to build?

**Carla:** Systematically? Not really, not across the board. It varies with the product owner, with who their counterpart on the business side is, with the product. Quite often someone has an idea and it goes straight into the backlog and into building, and nobody has asked whether it's the right problem in the first place.

**Interviewer:** And when someone does build a useful helper, a prompt or a skill, where does it end up?

**Carla:** Nowhere shared, as far as I know. Years ago somebody started a sort of collection, a wiki page, I think. I don't believe it's still around. I'd have to ask.

**Interviewer:** What I see a lot is that individuals get very far very fast, and the team as a whole lags, because what one person learns stays on their laptop. The company's knowledge should sit somewhere everyone can reach it.

**Carla:** Mm. That's all interesting.

**Interviewer:** If you had to name the one gap that matters most for your product owners, what would it be?

**Carla:** Not AI, funnily enough. Authority. Whether a product owner and their business counterpart can actually decide something, the two of them, without running around getting every step signed off by others. We've put a lot into that. And it still comes down to the individuals. And outside my area, product skills are just thin in this company. I've more or less stopped trying to change that.

**Interviewer:** Stopped?

**Carla:** Mostly. I pick where my energy goes.

**Interviewer:** What about your own AI use?

**Carla:** Too little. I know I should be putting more of my own time into it. I'm okay with how we're set up right now, but I think the real shake-up is still ahead of us. Engineering will feel it first, and not long after it lands on the product owners' desks, and I'd rather they weren't standing there surprised. So, specifically: do you have a training format for product owners? Something around AI and discovery, that's roughly what I had in mind.

**Interviewer:** We do, a couple. Before I describe them, what do you think your people would need?

**Carla:** Hm. What have you got? Tell me what's on the menu and I'll react to it.

**Interviewer:** Roughly two directions. One starts with the setup and the basics, so everyone works with the tools properly. The other goes into discovery work with AI, on their real work.

**Carla:** Both sound interesting. If I had to put them in an order, setup and the basics on the tool first.

**Interviewer:** Why that order?

**Carla:** Because I worry about the ones at the back. That some people fall behind and don't dare to ask, and then they just drift. And we have nothing in-house to catch them, no mentoring, nothing like that. Oh, and there's one complication. The coding agent the developers use, non-developers had access to it, and now that's being taken away again. I'm pushing back on that, and not gently. But until the security side is sorted, I wouldn't want a training built on that tool.

**Interviewer:** Do you know what the security concerns are?

**Carla:** Not in detail. That's being discussed somewhere above me. Can I ask you something, though? Given how far apart my teams are, would you mix everyone and hope the quick ones pull the others along? Or is it kinder to split them by where they stand, so nobody spends two days either waiting or drowning?

**Interviewer:** That depends on who exactly is in it. It's something we'd work out with you before anything is fixed.

**Carla:** Okay. And between the two directions you described I haven't decided either, so that's open as well. How many people make sense for one of these?

**Interviewer:** About ten per trainer works well. Above that we bring a second person.

**Carla:** And on site or remote?

**Interviewer:** We prefer on site.

**Carla:** That would be possible, we have the space. How much lead time do you need?

**Interviewer:** Not much. And it's better if it runs on your own work rather than on a made-up example.

**Carla:** Here's what happens on my side, then: I'll take this to two colleagues and talk it through with them. No rush, though. The rest of the year is full, nothing will happen before the holidays.

**Interviewer:** Should I send you a couple of example agendas, so you have something to show them?

**Carla:** Sure, why not. Send them over. Right, I should go back in. They're doing the summary round next door and they'll notice the empty chair.

**Interviewer:** Go. Thanks, Carla, this was really helpful.

**Carla:** Thank you. We'll talk in the new year.

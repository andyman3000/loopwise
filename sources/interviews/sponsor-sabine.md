# Sponsor interview: Sabine, head of digital product, electronics retailer

- **Basis:** synthetic, built from patterns in real conversations
- **Format:** one-on-one interview, full conversation
- **Date:** Autumn
- **Interviewee:** Sabine Kröger (she/her), head of digital product at an electronics retailer with about 200 stores; after a booked workshop, with a second group booked
- **Interviewer:** Loopwise

---

**Interviewer:** Hi Sabine. Thanks for fitting this in before the second group starts.

**Sabine:** Hi. Fine by me, I've got the ticket system in the other window, so if I go quiet I'm reading something I shouldn't be.

**Interviewer:** Busy week?

**Sabine:** Planning week. Everybody wants their thing in next quarter, and my job this week is mostly saying no. So this is a nice break, honestly.

**Interviewer:** Good. Today I'm looking backwards, not ahead: how the first run looked from your side, from the first call to the end of day two. Is it okay if I take notes?

**Sabine:** Go ahead.

**Interviewer:** How did the whole thing end up on your desk?

**Sabine:** Handed over. A colleague got it going, the first contact with you and so on, and then he passed it to me. So now I'm the one who organises the training group. On top of the day job.

**Interviewer:** Remind me of the day job.

**Sabine:** I head digital product. The teams behind the online shop and the app, and some of what runs in the stores. And I've kept one product for myself, which people find odd for someone in my role. It's the service that knows which accessories and spare parts fit which device. Chargers, cases, the filters for the coffee machines.

**Interviewer:** What does that look like for the people using it?

**Sabine:** It doesn't look like anything. There's nothing you could click on. Other systems ask it a question, it answers, and the shop and the app show the result. My users are the integration colleagues who call it.

**Interviewer:** And who builds it?

**Sabine:** An external partner. I don't have developers of my own on it.

**Interviewer:** Before the first run we sent you a list of questions. The last one was what would have to happen in the two days for you to say it was worth it. Do you remember what you wrote?

**Sabine:** Roughly. The selfish part first. I wanted to build something myself. I spend my days describing things for other people to build, so that was the bit I was looking forward to most.

**Interviewer:** And the other part?

**Sabine:** The bigger picture. If AI sits in the middle of how software gets made, from the idea to the release, which steps shift, and where do you trip. I wanted to see that once, the whole way through.

**Interviewer:** That's for you. Did you write anything for the group?

**Sabine:** No, now that you mention it. I answered as me, as a product owner. Not for them.

**Interviewer:** And if we'd asked how you'd tell afterwards that it had worked for them?

**Sabine:** Then you'd have got a shrug. Even my own answer had more question marks in it than goals, if I'm honest. For the group I'd probably have pointed at the agenda and said, if they get through that, good.

**Interviewer:** What did you think the group needed?

**Sabine:** We covered that in the first call. My people are product owners and analysts, a couple of team leads. Not developers. You laid out the arc you had in mind, quicker at the work they already do, and by the end a small agent of their own. I said fine.

**Interviewer:** And for your own product?

**Sabine:** For me it was narrower. My partner's developers aren't allowed to code with AI. So whatever I take away from this has to show up in what I hand them. Clearer requirements, basically.

**Interviewer:** Who was in the first group?

**Sabine:** You've got the list. On the technical side there were just two, our architect and someone who runs a project in IT. Everyone else works on requirements, like me.

**Interviewer:** And their experience with AI?

**Sabine:** Anything from people who'd asked a chat assistant something twice, to people who already had the coding agent running and did proper work with it. Your preparation sheet put us somewhere between your first two descriptions, and I didn't argue.

**Interviewer:** You also sent us a participant list.

**Sabine:** Name, role, product. That took longer than I expected. Most of them I know well. But two or three I'd only ever seen in big meetings, so for those I wrote down my best guess of what they work on.

**Interviewer:** And the other questions on our list?

**Sabine:** A good part of those I couldn't answer. Can people read the code of their products? Can you get a repository to work in? Can they install things? No idea. I sent those on to our architect and hoped. Read access to the code I doubted, to be honest. Not without somebody setting it up.

**Interviewer:** Let's stay with setup. That took a while, if I remember.

**Sabine:** It did. We're careful with AI here. Before anyone uses it for anything, the use case has to be registered first. And of the tools you work with, exactly one is cleared: the coding agent, inside the code editor. The browser version and the desktop app, no.

**Interviewer:** Even though people had licences.

**Sabine:** Even though. We do have a chat assistant from another vendor that everybody got, and people use that one. Just not the one you train with.

**Interviewer:** How did people get the coding agent installed?

**Sabine:** IT's route was a script you run on the command line. On Windows you need extra developer tooling before it even starts. For my analysts that is not a normal morning. You'd said in the first call that a command line would scare people off before we'd begun, so I was in touch with the service desk for a while. In the end I got it installed properly together with a couple of colleagues.

**Interviewer:** Anything else that got in the way during the two days?

**Sabine:** Little things. The shared folder, for one. You were external, so every file had to be shared with you individually, it never works for the whole folder. I ended up going through them one by one.

**Interviewer:** And real work data? What were people allowed to put in?

**Sabine:** When we ran it, that wasn't settled. Tickets, epics, notes from talking to users, nobody had said yes or no. It was with IT. And most of what we know lives in the ticket system and the wiki anyway. Whether the agent was allowed to look in there was still an open question when we started.

**Interviewer:** You'd asked everyone to bring a real problem. Did you bring one?

**Sabine:** I did. It didn't get built in the end, the group went with other cases. But mine is one of those jobs that make you sigh.

**Interviewer:** Go on.

**Sabine:** Whenever we take on a new brand, someone opens the manufacturer's compatibility tables and types them into our product system, line by line. I'd love a small service that does the copying. That's what I brought.

**Interviewer:** What about the prototyping part? In the first call it sounded like it might not be for you.

**Sabine:** It didn't look like it. A product with no screen, developers who aren't allowed to use the tool. Though I'd done one small thing already, before the workshop. The integration colleagues check my service's answers in the tool they use for calling interfaces, and I'd had the chat assistant help me make a little visual of those answers right in there. That worked. But that was the extent of it.

**Interviewer:** And then in the workshop?

**Sabine:** Then a colleague from a neighbouring area gave me a job. He gets asked all the time why an item isn't showing in the shop when there's stock for it. So he said, build me something where I type in an item number and see where it's live. He was my user, and he answered my questions while I built it.

**Interviewer:** How long did it take?

**Sabine:** Less than I'd have thought. The first version was all white, like a form from the nineties, because I'd tried to grab a screenshot of our shop for the look and my laptop simply gave up. The changes he asked for over lunch took about a minute. So in the break I added our colours and the logo as well.

**Interviewer:** And when you showed it?

**Sabine:** It found something right there in the demo. I typed in an item, and the stock was there, but express delivery wasn't switched on for it. I wasn't looking for that, I was just clicking through.

**Interviewer:** What did your colleague make of it?

**Sabine:** He was pleased, but his take was that the screen itself was nothing special. Everything on it had been sitting in our systems the whole time. Nobody had ever pulled it up in one place and looked.

**Interviewer:** Will it go anywhere?

**Sabine:** That's not my call. It reads data that belongs to another team, the people who own the product master data. They'd have to want it.

**Interviewer:** So what would you do with it?

**Sabine:** Nothing new has to be built for it, so I wouldn't go in with a request. I'd put the screen up at the start of their next planning meeting and let them react to their own data.

**Interviewer:** At the end of day two, what stuck with you?

**Sabine:** The prototyping, by a long way. What I keep thinking about is how the item lookup got made: him next to me, saying what he needed, and the thing growing while he talked. I can see myself doing that with the integration colleagues. One of them has a wish, we book an afternoon and leave with something running.

**Interviewer:** Why does that matter to you?

**Sabine:** Because here you don't win people over with a document. You win them with something they can click on. And then I'd have something in my hand when I go around asking for support.

**Interviewer:** How did the pairs work out, in the first run?

**Sabine:** You'd asked me to think about pairs beforehand, people from the same product area. In the end you did it on the first morning yourselves. Everyone gave themselves a number for how much they'd used AI, and you started by putting the top end with the bottom end. I was at the top end.

**Interviewer:** And the mix of products, did that matter?

**Sabine:** The products are really different. Some teams sit right in the shop front end, others run services in the background like mine. Search, product pages, delivery tracking, trade-in. They don't run on the same systems.

**Interviewer:** With a group like that, did you have a picture of how the two days should be set up?

**Sabine:** Not really. I could tell you who was coming, and roughly how far along each of them was. What that meant for two days, whether the ones who were further ahead would get anything out of it, or the others would keep up, I couldn't have said. Pairs were your idea, and the way you set it up worked, so I never had to find out.

**Interviewer:** Let's talk about how work reaches your teams. How does it usually come in?

**Sabine:** Often already solved. Someone from the business comes in with "we need a filter here, by Friday", and from then on the conversation is about the filter, not about the problem. Getting them off their solution is hard work.

**Interviewer:** Is there a step before that, where someone checks whether an idea is worth doing at all?

**Sabine:** Not as a habit. Some teams do, most don't. Ask why something got built and the honest answer is often that it seemed obvious at the time.

**Interviewer:** And the way the teams are set up?

**Sabine:** Take any of the big projects that touch everyone. When one comes along, the small teams get emptied into it, whatever the org chart says. The org chart says we're moving towards teams that have everything they need for one product. And most teams work agile on paper. How well, depends on the team.

**Interviewer:** And requirements?

**Sabine:** There's no shared way of doing them. Whether you ever talk to your stakeholders before you write something down is up to you. Nobody checks.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned the second group. How did that come about?

**Sabine:** We booked another round. A smaller group this time, the same two days.

**Interviewer:** Before this run, did you tell us what you'd want to be different?

**Sabine:** I sent you a list. A long one.

**Interviewer:** And then?

**Sabine:** Then we had a call, you put last time's agenda up with a few changes and asked if that works for us. And I said yes, it works.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything you'd push for?

**Sabine:** Not much. Spec writing stays out, like last time, and that suits me. A couple of people told me last time the skills part was the more important one for them anyway.

**Interviewer:** One practical thing. Our material and the prompts are in English. Is that okay for the second group?

**Sabine:** English is fine. The meetings most of them sit in are in English already, half the teams have colleagues from abroad. That this group happens to be all German is more chance than anything. And if two of them want to argue in German during an exercise, let them.

**Interviewer:** That's my list. Anything you'd like us to know before the second group?

**Sabine:** Only the setup. I'll put together the checklist for the new people again, what to install and how to log in, like last time. The rest you know better than I do.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Sabine. That was a lot of useful detail.

**Sabine:** Sure. Back to saying no to people, then.

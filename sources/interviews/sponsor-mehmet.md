# Sponsor interview: Mehmet, product director, B2B commerce software vendor

- **Basis:** synthetic, built from patterns in real conversations
- **Format:** one-on-one interview, full conversation
- **Date:** Autumn
- **Interviewee:** Mehmet Hoffmann (he/him), product director at a long-established vendor of B2B software for online shops and product catalogues; sponsor of a booked two-day workshop, interviewed in the days before it runs
- **Interviewer:** Loopwise

---

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time this close to the workshop. How are you feeling about it?

**Mehmet:** Mixed. Looking forward to it, and a bit nervous. I'm working from the kitchen table today because I wanted one quiet hour. My main worry is that we'll need more time in the room than we'd like.

**Interviewer:** We'll get to that. Can I take you back to the very start first? When you got in touch with us, what were you actually after?

**Mehmet:** Not a training. I pictured someone from outside who sits with the product people for a couple of months, two, maybe three, roughly a day a week. Someone who watches how they work, brings in some structure and good practice, and is there when somebody gets stuck.

**Interviewer:** Why not a classic training? A day or two on the basics?

**Mehmet:** Because I thought my people were past the basics. They'd been using these tools for a while. Nobody needed an introduction to what a prompt is.

**Interviewer:** How long had you been in the company back then?

**Mehmet:** A few weeks. Very new. I said it at the time, too: I found it hard to even see how the teams worked. Whether they shared any common standard or whether each one had its own way. I didn't have that overview.

**Interviewer:** Think of that very first message to us. Which of the usual questions could you have answered on the spot?

**Mehmet:** The shape I knew. The product people, a weekly rhythm, a couple of months. What I didn't have was a figure. No budget in my head, and no idea what my boss would approve. You offered to put something rough on paper with the basic outline, and I said fine, I'll take it to him and see how he reacts. The number came from your side.

**Interviewer:** Did you have a start in mind?

**Mehmet:** Nothing was settled on my side. The start date in that first offer was your proposal. Real dates only came much later, once we'd decided internally on the two-day format.

**Interviewer:** Right. How did it get from months to two days?

**Mehmet:** My boss found it too expensive. He didn't want to commit to months with someone we'd never worked with, so he said, let's do a smaller first step and see how working together goes. So it shrank: one prep day on your side, about a day and a half in the room with us. Which is a bit funny, because I'd said a short format would be too little for my people.

**Interviewer:** Once the two days were set, what were they supposed to be about?

**Mehmet:** At first, building. The product people had collected a long list of ideas for small AI helpers. The brief was: pick a handful, build them in the two days, and start one shared place where they live.

**Interviewer:** Is that still the plan?

**Mehmet:** No, I changed it a few weeks later. I sat down with our head of engineering to map how work actually travels from an idea to something his people can build, and while we were drawing it, the chain itself became the interesting part. So now we take one piece of work and run it once through the whole thing, from where the idea comes from to a handover engineering can pick up. If helpers come out of it, good, but they're a by-product.

**Interviewer:** Some sponsors like participants to bring their own daily problems into a workshop like that. Did you consider it?

**Mehmet:** I didn't want it. If everybody brings their own little issue from last Tuesday, we spend the day on ten private things. I'd rather prepare the run-through properly beforehand and leave the one-off problems out.

**Interviewer:** Let me step back. Across the whole development process, where does it hurt most today?

**Mehmet:** I can't name one bottleneck. It's not that specs are always late or anything like that. What's missing is a shared way of working. There isn't one.

**Interviewer:** What does that look like in a normal week?

**Mehmet:** Everyone builds their own thing. Somebody makes a clever setup for writing tickets, shows it in a meeting, people nod, and then they all go back to their desks and carry on alone. Showing each other works fine. Thinking something through together, that's what doesn't happen.

**Interviewer:** And if it worked the way you'd like?

**Mehmet:** Then one person's approach gets picked up by the next person, who bends it a bit for their own work. And slowly we'd build a shared map of where AI actually helps along our process. One step at a time, everybody tries a step before we move to the next.

**Interviewer:** Why did you think that needed someone from outside?

**Mehmet:** Because among themselves they don't do it. I believed a neutral person whose only job is to work alongside them would get them to actually share ideas and agree on things.

**Interviewer:** How is engineering doing with all this, by comparison?

**Mehmet:** Far ahead. They did a short training together, all of them, and their leads steer the change as a group. There's much more talk between them. I want the product side to get there.

**Interviewer:** Does that create a tempo problem between the two sides?

**Mehmet:** There's a tempo problem, but a different one. At the top of the company the expectation is now: much faster, soon. And when I ask the team leads who'd have to deliver it, they tell me they've barely tried it on real work and can't say yet what a normal week with it looks like.

**Interviewer:** So you're somewhere in between.

**Mehmet:** A bit. One thing I won't do is go out and promise a number, like we'll be so many times quicker. I think that's a bad idea.

**Interviewer:** You're not from engineering yourself, are you?

**Mehmet:** No, I'm not a developer. And this company is engineering all the way through, very good engineering. But making things pleasant to use, saying clearly what we want to achieve, explaining ourselves to people outside, those were never our strengths, and people here know it. Part of why they brought me in is to lead in that direction.

**Interviewer:** Where do the product owners come from, background-wise?

**Mehmet:** One of the engineering colleagues says most of them came from the technical side, and that this leaves a gap the current change could close. I'm not sure it's that simple. A colleague who's been here much longer describes them as a more mixed group.

**Interviewer:** Mixed in what way?

**Mehmet:** According to that colleague, they started from very different places, they don't talk to each other much, and they weren't part of the basic agent training the developers did.

**Interviewer:** Let's talk about success. How does the company tell today whether a feature works?

**Mehmet:** Badly, if I'm honest. We don't really have product KPIs anyone steers by. There's some basic analytics and some testing, both fairly new. And our customers measure their own shops themselves, so that data sits with them. For the new assistant I've started looking at how many people come back to it in the second and third week after trying it, and I think I'm the first one doing that. I'd rather not go into the figures. Putting them on the table feels a little like pointing at colleagues.

**Interviewer:** Could the company goals serve as a frame for the workshop example?

**Mehmet:** They're very high level. Hard to steer one example by them. The goal I keep coming back to myself is closer to the ground: getting the assistant live with its first customers who actually pay for it, not just test it.

**Interviewer:** But the company goals would probably do as a rough backdrop, wouldn't they?

**Mehmet:** Yes, sure. That'll work.

**Interviewer:** And the two days themselves? When would you say they worked?

**Mehmet:** The aim isn't a fully specified feature. It's learning where our process is strong and where it's weak. In practice: a few real examples taken through to the handover, with what we produced along the way lying on the table, and early learning about which steps hold.

**Interviewer:** And what would failure look like?

**Mehmet:** The picture I have is the next morning at the coffee machine: one person describes our process, and another says, no, that's not what we agreed. Everybody left with a different version. That's the one I fear most. Otherwise, too much lecturing, or nothing handed over in the form we agreed.

**Interviewer:** Who's in the room?

**Mehmet:** Ten, eleven people. The product owners from here, the product person from our second site, a designer, the head of engineering, an architect or two. My boss, who runs product and technology together. And the CEO.

**Interviewer:** The CEO as well.

**Mehmet:** Yes, and that's a risk, I'll say it myself. He thinks years ahead, which is his job. But in the workshop we have to stay with what works today, and I don't want him pulling the group toward where things might be one day. We agreed that he and my boss take part like everyone else.

**Interviewer:** Did your boss set any conditions?

**Mehmet:** One rule. The process has to be usable by anyone in the company, not only technical people. So the early steps can't sit somewhere only engineers work. What engineering does at its own end is up to them.

**Interviewer:** How would you describe the level of the group?

**Mehmet:** Fairly advanced. I don't think the spread is a big issue.

**Interviewer:** Has everyone used the coding agent?

**Mehmet:** Not everyone. Some have. A few have their own personal setup file, most don't. The chat tool, everyone has used that.

**Interviewer:** How did you get a read on that?

**Mehmet:** A colleague and I looked at usage of the chat tool. A handful of heavy users, lots of light ones. Then someone objected that the product owners mostly work in a different assistant, so those numbers don't say much. We sent round a short questionnaire instead.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned being nervous about time. Where does that come from?

**Mehmet:** Partly it's just me. I keep thinking we'll run out of time in there. I've said it in two of our calls already, I know.

**Interviewer:** How was last week, with the prep?

**Mehmet:** Heavy. Every evening there was another long document, and each one assumed I'd already worked through the one before. What I asked for was some ground under my feet: either a few pieces stop moving so I can build on them, or someone takes part of the work off me.

**Interviewer:** Was it mainly the volume?

**Mehmet:** Not mainly. We'd talked about this chain for weeks, and not once had anyone actually run it with real content from one step to the next. I wanted that done before the workshop. Otherwise the whole group, with the senior people sitting there, starts a fundamental debate about the process from scratch.

**Interviewer:** Did everyone on your side agree?

**Mehmet:** No. Our head of engineering sees it the other way round: for him the workshop is exactly the moment to play it through. And some of my push is probably just last-minute nerves, I know that.

**Interviewer:** Did you try parts of it yourself?

**Mehmet:** I did. Broadly, it works, which was a relief. The hard part is writing the hypotheses. And the material kept pointing to files we don't have, which will confuse people in the room, so I asked for those references to go.

**Interviewer:** Which step was hardest for you?

**Mehmet:** The structured problem statement from a blank page. Even without stacking up assumptions, it was hard for me. I doubt every role in that room can do it, so I wanted a simpler first step that anybody can fill in.

**Interviewer:** And where does the example start? Where does the idea come from?

**Mehmet:** We wanted real customer signals, what people write to support. That fell through: the support system isn't connected, and nobody had time to pull the data out by hand. So I sat down with the product owners from both sites, we agreed on a few small cases from the new assistant, and I wrote a first assumption for each. It's a prepared stand-in, and we'll say so openly in the room.

**Interviewer:** How do you keep a case realistic without it getting too heavy?

**Mehmet:** Our head of engineering raised exactly that. He's seen sessions where the example dragged in so much of the old system that people walked out sure none of it could work here. So it needs a balance. He added, half joking, that this is what we have an outsider for, and took it back in the same breath.

**Interviewer:** Once the two days are over, what could still go wrong?

**Mehmet:** That's my bigger fear, actually. Two good days, we'll manage. What worries me is the Monday after, and the one after that, when the next deadline comes and nobody's checking whether we still work the new way.

**Interviewer:** Has anyone else raised that?

**Mehmet:** Our head of engineering. He says he's seen it often: people do a session like this and then slide back into their old habits. He asked me which metrics would show whether the new process is actually being used.

**Interviewer:** What's your plan for afterwards?

**Mehmet:** Short check-ins at regular intervals, and then one longer session some weeks later where we go deeper.

**Interviewer:** With us?

**Mehmet:** I assume that would be work for you, but nothing is commissioned. I mentioned it to my boss. He was busy with something else and said, sure, it has to continue.

**Interviewer:** Who carries it on your side after the workshop?

**Mehmet:** Me. I haven't named anyone else. The product colleague at our second site is fully briefed, but carrying it, that's me.

**Interviewer:** A longer view to finish. Do you see agents doing the product work at some point?

**Mehmet:** Not the early discovery part, not for a good while. Someone still has to kick it off, and the tool has no idea that this one customer is the one we can't afford to upset. Your colleague put it more sharply in one of our prep calls: that front part is judgement, and there's no unit test for judgement. Where the tools will be in a year or two, nobody knows. The way of working and the habits, we have to build those now either way.

**Interviewer:** That's a good place to stop. Thank you for your time.

**Mehmet:** Thanks. I'll send the case list over tonight so you have it before the weekend. See you in the room.

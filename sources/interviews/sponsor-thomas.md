# Sponsor interview: Thomas, VP product, property-management software vendor

- **Basis:** synthetic, built from patterns in real conversations
- **Format:** one-on-one interview, full conversation
- **Date:** Autumn
- **Interviewee:** Thomas Reinhardt (he/him), VP product at a vendor of software for property managers; booked a two-day in-person workshop for his product group, before it runs
- **Interviewer:** Loopwise

---

**Interviewer:** Thomas, hi. Are you in a car?

**Thomas:** Parked. I'm outside the dentist's, early for once in my life, so you have me for about forty minutes and then I belong to someone with a drill.

**Interviewer:** That works. Thanks for squeezing it in. The workshop is booked and the dates are fixed, so this isn't about logistics. I'd like to understand your group and what you hope changes. Who's coming?

**Thomas:** People from my area. A bit under twenty: product managers, product owners, a few business analysts, and one UX designer, who asked whether she's allowed, and I said of course.

**Interviewer:** And the list is final?

**Thomas:** Ha. No. The two days, those we settled almost in the first week, the dates too. The names kept moving. The count went up and down by two or three until very recently, and one person got added who wasn't on my first list at all: the colleague who runs the company's AI programme. He asked late, and it made sense to have him.

**Interviewer:** I'd like to come back to him. First, how do PMs and POs split the work at your company?

**Thomas:** PMs own the direction, where a product goes, and they live in the roadmap tool. POs are the operational half, they live in the ticket system. That's the theory, anyway.

**Interviewer:** And how does a roadmap come together?

**Thomas:** PMs and POs draft it, then they sit down with the developers, who estimate it and commit to it together with them. After that it's really the POs and the developers who carry it through.

**Interviewer:** Does every team do that the same way?

**Thomas:** No, and that's the honest answer. If you moved a PO from one team to the next, she'd have to relearn where things get written down, who signs off, what "ready" means. We're setting up a common standard right now. That's a project of its own, it would be on my desk with or without AI.

**Interviewer:** Where is the standard coming from?

**Thomas:** Partly from the leadership group. Over the last year we rebuilt most of the product and engineering management layer. Several of the new product heads came in from outside and they know what good product work looks like. Below them, a lot of the PMs and POs have never worked that way.

**Interviewer:** If you had to name the biggest weakness in product work today, what would it be?

**Thomas:** Method. We don't share methods, and nobody here could draw you the road from "someone has an idea" to "it's live". If AI didn't exist I'd give you the same answer.

**Interviewer:** Is that road drawn anywhere?

**Thomas:** There's a diagram. It even has a scoring step and a discovery step in it, which people like to point at in meetings. What it doesn't have yet is anyone using it. We're only now rolling it out to the teams, one at a time.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned scoring. How do priorities get set today?

**Thomas:** Gut and urgency. Every request arrives labelled as top priority, so in effect nothing is. We picked a scoring method, it sits in that diagram, nobody uses it. And then decisions on individual requirements are slow, because you can't rank a pile of number ones.

**Interviewer:** What about the requirements themselves? What does a typical ticket look like?

**Thomas:** For the big product document there's a template, and people fill it in, more or less. One level down, a feature, a ticket, there's nothing. No template, and no agreement on what a good one even is.

**Interviewer:** So what ends up in them?

**Thomas:** Often not a lot. A title, two lines, sometimes the description is empty. And if you ask, the answer is that everyone involved knows what's meant. Which is true, right up until the person building it wasn't part of those conversations. Then it comes apart.

**Interviewer:** Is that getting better or worse?

**Thomas:** It's about to matter much more. One of our engineering teams is building a new product, I'll spare you what it does, and that team is our pilot for agent-based development. Once the agents build quickly, the slow part is us. Our input. If product doesn't speed up its own half, we get run over. And most of my people don't see that coming yet.

**Interviewer:** What does engineering actually want from product?

**Thomas:** Clear requirements with acceptance criteria. That's the first thing out of their mouths every time I ask. And then there's a second thing they tell me, which stings a bit. That many PMs have no picture of where their product should go. They're stuck in the day to day, or in how it's always been done, and whatever a customer or sales sends over gets passed straight on without anyone checking it.

**Interviewer:** How does the pilot team come into the workshop?

**Thomas:** Its PM and its PO are both coming, and if I'm honest, that pair is the one I care about most. The PO already went through the engineering training on the agent tools. The pilot is meant to be the first team that works agent-based from end to end, and what product hands it is still too thin.

**Interviewer:** You said "about to matter." Is there a history behind that?

**Thomas:** There is. For a long time product planned more than it delivered. Quarter after quarter, more went in at the top than came out at the bottom. People have watched that gap long enough that there's not much patience left. And now AI is supposed to close it, quickly, while the basics underneath aren't in place.

**Interviewer:** Is the organisation ready for that?

**Thomas:** No. And we can't wait until it is, either. That's the uncomfortable part. The tools won't repair what's structural. If there's no road from idea to release, an agent won't lay one for us.

**Interviewer:** When people at your company say "agent-driven", what do they mean, in practice?

**Thomas:** Good question, and I don't have a clean answer. We say it a lot, we want to get there fast. What it would look like for one of our PMs on an ordinary Tuesday, nobody has actually worked through. Me included.

**Interviewer:** Then what would make the two days a success for you?

**Thomas:** Small is fine. If people get a bit better at deciding and a bit better at writing requirements, even one notch, I'd count that as a win.

**Interviewer:** And how would you see it, afterwards?

**Thomas:** Two ways. Whether they use what they learned in a normal week, and whether they show the person next to them. That's the one I'll watch. If you want a number: cycle time. If the time from idea to shipped gets shorter, that buys time for discovery, which is where we're thinnest.

**Interviewer:** And what would failure look like?

**Thomas:** Two versions. People go back to their desks and file AI under "interesting, but not for my kind of work". Or nothing changes in how we do product, because we spent the two days on a narrow list of use cases instead of on the whole job.

**Interviewer:** A narrow list. Is there a particular one?

**Thomas:** Our AI programme lead has one. As part of his programme he's collected AI use cases for product work. Some of it is good.

**Interviewer:** What did he bring into the planning when he joined?

**Thomas:** He told us that management expects a big jump in efficiency right after the workshop. Not next year, right after. And he pushed for things people can use for real work the moment they're back at their desks.

**Interviewer:** How do you weigh that against the programme we'd outlined?

**Thomas:** Your programme stays the frame. His use cases go in where they fit. If we just work down his list, we've trained people on a list, not on product management.

**Interviewer:** Is there something that bridges his expectation and what two days can deliver?

**Thomas:** I think so. If the group goes home with one or two assistants or skills already set up in our company platform, things they really keep using afterwards, then there's something to show, and we haven't turned the workshop into a tool demo.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the tools, then.

**Thomas:** There's one company-wide chat and agent platform, the standard for everybody. Then the roadmap tool, which has its own AI features. The ticket system. A design tool on the UX side. And just recently we gave the participants licences for a coding assistant, so they'd have it before the workshop.

**Interviewer:** How much do people use any of it?

**Thomas:** Nearly all of them use the AI that's built into their tools, a little. Summarise this, tidy up that. I haven't met anyone who's really against it. Most are open and just don't know how to make it part of their day.

**Interviewer:** And the spread across the room?

**Thomas:** Wide. One or two know a lot: the pilot PO from the engineering training, and our AI programme lead, who has built a fair amount himself. Then the big middle, who only touch the built-in features. Whatever level the two days aim at, it won't suit everybody in there.

**Interviewer:** Did that spread happen by accident?

**Thomas:** No, I built it that way. I've got a PO who thinks her job is writing tickets sitting next to one who thinks he's running a small company. Someone ten years in next to someone in month three. And they plan their weeks in completely different ways. That mix is the point.

**Interviewer:** Back to the coding assistant. Does it stay after the workshop?

**Thomas:** Still open. Either they keep it or they move to the company platform. I'll pay for a tool that shows it earns its keep. I won't hand everyone every tool just because it exists.

**Interviewer:** How do the platform and your other tools connect?

**Thomas:** Barely. The platform can't write into the ticket system, and it has no link to the roadmap tool at all. I'd rather have one single source of truth that agents work from. I've started an argument internally about whether we need both the roadmap tool and the ticket system. Not settled.

**Interviewer:** What's in the roadmap tool that an agent would want?

**Thomas:** More than you'd guess. Personas, for one, and decent ones. Ask a room of my people where to find them and you'd get maybe two hands.

**Interviewer:** Where else does context live? Documentation?

**Thomas:** Spread out. A wiki here, a shared drive there, a lot in people's heads. None of it sits next to the code. Actually, one result I'd value from the two days is a list: what we have to put in place, documentation, context that people and agents can reach, before agent-based work is real for us.

**Interviewer:** Do your colleagues see it that way?

**Thomas:** Some seem to think this way of working will turn up on its own once the licences are paid for. It won't. Somebody has to build it.

**Interviewer:** What about the customer side? Where does customer input come in?

**Thomas:** That's a sore spot. There's nothing structured. Support runs in several separate ways depending on the product. We're building a way to collect feedback, it isn't there yet. Usage tracking exists in one product, in the others nothing.

**Interviewer:** And prototypes? Do you show ideas to users before building?

**Thomas:** Not really, no. I think. Maybe one team does it quietly, but I don't know of anyone putting a prototype in front of a property manager before we build.

**Interviewer:** When a big piece of discovery comes up, who gets it?

**Thomas:** Not necessarily us. When something big and urgent lands, product isn't the obvious first choice. People here don't quite believe we'd get it done, at least not quickly. That tells you where we stand.

**Interviewer:** Let me ask a narrow one. If everyone could take a single skill out of the two days, what should it be?

**Thomas:** Having the AI write PRDs and requirements. That one.

**Interviewer:** Only that?

**Thomas:** As one skill, yes. The bigger wish behind it is something else: an assistant that decides based on facts and turns out complete requirements, with acceptance criteria, every time.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything about how people use AI right now that bothers you?

**Thomas:** One thing, more than most things at the moment. Some people fill the gaps in their experience with whatever the chat hands them, and then present it as their own expertise. You hear a very sure answer in a meeting and you can tell where it came from.

**Interviewer:** Why does that get to you so much?

**Thomas:** Because underneath, the person still hasn't learned it. A faster car doesn't teach you the way. If you don't know the job, the agent only gets you to the wrong place sooner.

**Interviewer:** What should people work on in the room?

**Thomas:** Their own real cases. Not an invented webshop. What's on their desk.

**Interviewer:** Any you have in mind?

**Thomas:** One of the product heads has a case I'd love to see. An existing feature in one of our products, works fine, nobody ever wrote down what it's supposed to do, and everyone who built it has left. He has to rebuild it and needs a way to work out from the outside what the requirements were.

**Interviewer:** After the workshop, what happens to the templates and processes you have today?

**Thomas:** People may change them, and I'd encourage it. The aim is shared standards across the company, and teams are welcome to bend them to how they work. Shared, not identical.

**Interviewer:** And what do you want from us once the two days are over?

**Thomas:** An honest read. After two days with my people you'll see things I don't. How mature is our product management really, and how ready are we for agent-based work. I have my own view and I want to check it against yours. Don't sand it down.

**Interviewer:** We'll write it up straight. Last thing: is there anyone in the group I should speak to before the two days?

**Thomas:** Not beforehand, no. You'll meet them all soon enough. And the receptionist has just come out and is waving at me, so that's my cue.

**Interviewer:** Then go. Good luck in the chair.

**Thomas:** Thanks. Talk soon.
